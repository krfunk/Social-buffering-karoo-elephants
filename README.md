
## Sociality modulates context-dependent stress responses to anthropogenic disturbance in managed African elephants 

Authors:
  * Kyla R. Funk
  * Levi Newediuk
  * Leslie R. Brown
  * Andre Ganswindt
  * Eric Vander Wal
  
This repository contains the accompanying code for "Sociality modulates context-dependent stress responses to anthropogenic disturbance in managed African elephants"

# Abstract

Understanding how social and environmental conditions jointly shape physiological stress is critical for predicting individual responses to disturbance, particularly in managed populations. We evaluated whether social and environmental stressors act additively or whether social context modifies physiological responses to environmental challenges. We combined four years of individual-based behavioural observations, social network data, and two years of faecal glucocorticoid metabolite samples from a small, intensively monitored population of African elephants (Loxodonta africana) in a fenced reserve. We quantified dominance rank and social network metrics and used a Bayesian framework to test how these factors interacted with variations in the biophysical environment, including natural environmental and anthropogenic disturbances, to predict glucocorticoid concentrations. Natural environmental variables, including weather, water availability, and season, did not predict glucocorticoid levels. In contrast, helicopter use, a common management intervention, was associated with elevated glucocorticoid concentrations. Social effects were context dependent. Following disturbance, low-ranking individuals showed higher glucocorticoid concentrations than high-ranking individuals, whereas this pattern reversed under low-disturbance conditions. Individuals with more social connections had lower glucocorticoid concentrations, particularly during disturbance periods, consistent with a buffering effect of social relationships. Measures of betweenness centrality did not predict glucocorticoid levels. These results demonstrate that social structure mediates physiological responses to anthropogenic disturbance and that management actions can impose uneven physiological costs across individuals. Integrating the physiological response to the interaction between social structure and the biophysical environment can guide conservation and management strategies and may improve the effectiveness of interventions in social species.
